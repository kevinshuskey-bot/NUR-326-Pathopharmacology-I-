Patho Review / Topic 8

Pain & Seizures

Nociception and the three analgesic classes built around it, plus how a seizure focus starts and spreads and what the anti-epileptic drugs actually do about it.

Pain physiology

Nociception = transduction (painful stimuli → action potential) → transmission (A-delta and C fibers carry the signal to spinal cord/brain) → perception → modulation.

FiberSpeed / localization
A-deltaMyelinated, rapid, well-localized pain
C fibersUnmyelinated, slow, poorly-localized pain

Prostaglandins lower the pain threshold and drive inflammation/fever — the target of NSAID therapy. Gate control theory: blocking pain signals before they reach the brain (touch, massage, distraction) lowers perceived pain.

NociceptiveNeuropathic
OriginActual/impending tissue injury (visceral or somatic)Direct nerve injury
FeelCramping, aching, well- or poorly-localized depending on typeShooting, burning, electric-shock, numb
ExamplesKidney stones, fractures, appendicitisDiabetic neuropathy, postherpetic neuralgia, phantom limb

Non-opioid analgesics

NSAIDs — COX-1 vs. COX-2

COX-1: protects gastric mucosa, needed for thromboxane/platelet activation. COX-2: drives inflammation and fever. Non-selective NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen, ASA) block both → GI ulcer risk + antiplatelet effect. Celecoxib blocks COX-2 only — spares the GI mucosa and platelets, but carries a black box cardiovascular thrombotic risk.

Aspirin specifics

Never give aspirin to a child with a viral illness — risk of Reye's syndrome (brain/liver damage, high mortality). Salicylate toxicity: tinnitus, N/V, seizures.

Ketorolac (Toradol): most potent NSAID, IV/IM, post-op use only, max 5 days — similar analgesia to morphine without respiratory depression, but higher renal risk.

Acetaminophen (Tylenol)

No anti-inflammatory effect, no COX/platelet effect. Max 4 g/24 hr (count combination products like Percocet/Norco). Overdose → hepatic necrosis via glutathione depletion; antidote acetylcysteine (Mucomyst). Limit to <2 g/day in chronic alcohol users.

Opioids

DrugKey points
MorphineMu agonist; PO/IV/epidural/rectal; watch respiratory & CNS depression, constipation
Hydromorphone (Dilaudid)~7× more potent than morphine (1 mg IV Dilaudid ≈ 7 mg IV morphine)
FentanylExtremely potent (0.1 mg IV ≈ 10 mg IV morphine); PO/IV/IM/transdermal
Meperidine (Demerol)Toxic metabolite with repeated doses → seizure risk; do not use for multiple/scheduled doses
CodeineMild-moderate pain + antitussive; PO only; not for children <18 (life-threatening respiratory risk)
OxycodonePO only, ~10× more potent than codeine, high abuse potential (Percocet, OxyContin)
MethadoneFull synthetic, long half-life; used for opioid-use-disorder detox/maintenance
Naloxone (Narcan)

Binds opioid receptors without activating them. Onset <2 min; IV/IM/intranasal. Reverses respiratory depression and analgesia — watch for abrupt return of pain and rebound hypertension.

Universal nursing consideration: constipation is nearly universal with opioids — proactively treat with stool softeners/hydration; can progress to paralytic ileus.

Seizures

A seizure focus is a group of abnormal neurons (often scarred/gliotic) that fire spontaneously and can recruit neighboring neurons. Epilepsy = recurrent, unprovoked seizures with no reversible metabolic cause.

Generalized onset

Both hemispheres at once

Formerly "grand mal." Tonic-clonic (rigid contraction → alternating jerk/relax) and absence seizures.

Focal onset

One localized region

Subdivided by level of patient awareness during the seizure.

Prodrome/aura — subjective warning sign (smell, taste, sound, mood change) that hints at the seizure focus location.

Anti-epileptic drugs (AEDs)

Black box — all AEDs

Increased risk of suicidal ideation, worsening depression, and mood changes.

MOA (3 mechanisms): raise the seizure threshold, limit spread of the discharge, or slow nerve conduction — some also work via GABA. Most require therapeutic drug monitoring. Never stop abruptly — can precipitate seizures.

DrugNotes
Phenytoin (hydantoin)First-line for tonic-clonic/focal; gingival hyperplasia, hirsutism, "Dilantin facies," lots of adverse effects
Valproic acidGeneralized seizures; highly protein-bound (many interactions); contraindicated in liver disease; hepatotoxicity/pancreatitis
Topiramate (Topamax)Adjunct therapy; watch for visual changes/closed-angle glaucoma; interacts with contraceptives
Levetiracetam (Keppra)Adjunct for focal seizures; generally well-tolerated

Rapid seizure management: IV push benzodiazepines (diazepam, lorazepam) are the gold standard for active seizures.
