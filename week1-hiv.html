HIV: Patho & Pharmacology

A retrovirus that writes itself into the host's own DNA. You don't need to memorize individual drug names here — the emphasis is on the life cycle, the clinical course, and the treatment principles.

Definitions

  • HIVHuman Immunodeficiency Virus — a retrovirus that destroys CD4+ T cells. HIV-1 was discovered first and is most prevalent worldwide; HIV-2 is less pathogenic and largely confined to West Africa.
  • AIDSAcquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome — caused by HIV. Untreated HIV typically progresses to AIDS in 8–10 years, marked by severe immune system dysfunction.
  • RetrovirusA virus that uses the enzyme reverse transcriptase to translate its RNA into DNA.
Epidemiology snapshot
  • ~1.1 million new infections annually in the US
  • South Africa has the highest overall HIV prevalence
  • 76% of US adults/adolescents with HIV are men; Black men have the highest rates of new infection; men who have sex with men account for the most new and existing cases
  • New cases among women are increasing

Pathogenesis — how HIV takes over a cell

HIV targets the CD4 protein on T lymphocytes, monocytes, and macrophages — helper T lymphocytes are the primary target.

  1. The virus binds CD4 and fuses with the cell membrane to get inside.
  2. Reverse transcriptase copies the viral RNA into DNA.
  3. HIV integrase inserts that DNA copy into the host cell's own genetic material.
  4. The infected cell is directed to synthesize and assemble new virus.
  5. HIV protease completes final assembly of virus particles, which are released to infect more cells.
HIV replicates prolifically — roughly 10 billion new particles produced every day, completely overwhelming the body's defenses over time.Module 1 voiceover

Sequence of events & significance

  • HIV invades CD4+ cells and becomes part of the cell's own DNA → the individual is infected for life; virus is present in blood and body fluids.
  • Virus proliferates and sheds particles from infected cells.
  • The body forms anti-HIV antibodies — a marker of infection, but not protective.
  • Progressive destruction of helper T cells → compromised cell-mediated immunity → opportunistic infections and neoplasms as immune defense collapses.

Clinical course

Stage 1

Early infection (acute)

Rapid viral replication; not yet detectable on traditional lab tests; usually no symptoms — but the patient is highly infectious. As seroconversion occurs, antibodies become detectable and flu-like symptoms can appear for several weeks (typically 2–4 weeks post-infection).

Stage 2

Clinical latency (chronic)

Virus levels stabilize as the body fights the infection. Lasts 3–12 years untreated, or decades with treatment. Mostly asymptomatic or mild symptoms, but viral production continues, CD4+ count persistently drops, and the immune response becomes progressively less effective as viral load rises.

Stage 3

Symptomatic HIV → AIDS

Frequent viral mutation complicates drug therapy, which is why multi-drug regimens are required.

Diagnosing AIDS

Either of the following is diagnostic:

  1. An AIDS-defining condition is present (Kaposi's sarcoma, wasting syndrome, certain cancers, pervasive candidiasis, and others below) — or
  2. CD4 count < 200 cells/mm³, regardless of whether an AIDS-defining condition is present.
AIDS-defining illnesses

Bacterial: Mycobacterium (TB, avium), recurrent pneumonia, recurrent salmonella septicemia. Cancers: Kaposi's sarcoma, invasive cervical cancer, lymphomas. Other: wasting syndrome, encephalopathy, AIDS dementia complex, pervasive candidiasis.

Oral manifestations

Common as CD4+ counts fall (fungal, viral, bacterial, cancerous), and a higher-risk sign for progression to AIDS — oral hairy leukoplakia and periodontal disease are the classic findings. Oral assessment matters.

HIV-associated dementia

Poor concentration, mental slowness, forgetfulness/memory loss, behavior changes, word-finding difficulty, depression/withdrawal, and motor/speech/balance/visual problems. Historically affected 40–60% of patients; that rate has dropped significantly with modern treatment.

Transmission

Risk behaviors

Sexual & drug-related

Men having sex with men, injection drug use, male-to-male contact + IDU, heterosexual contact, mother-to-child (perinatal), other (transfusions, unknown).

Parenteral

Blood-borne

Needle/syringe sharing, blood/blood products, healthcare exposure to blood/body fluids/sharps.

Why doesn't every exposure cause infection? Duration/frequency of contact, volume/virulence/concentration of the virus, host immune status, and genetic protective factors all play a role.

Occupational exposure risk

Even with a needle-stick exposure to an HIV-positive source, the risk of transmission is still under 1%. Any exposure still requires reporting/documentation and evaluation — depending on risk level, post-exposure prophylaxis may be recommended.

Pharmacology: antiretroviral therapy (ART)

You do not need to memorize individual drug names for HIV pharmacology — but you do need the generalizations, since this is a rapidly evolving treatment area.

ART typically combines two nucleoside drugs + a third agent from a different class (3 drugs total). This combination has produced dramatic reductions in HIV morbidity/mortality and can keep patients in clinical latency for decades.

Six drug classes (generalizations only)
  • Fusion inhibitors & CCR5 antagonists — block viral entry into the cell
  • NRTIs (nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors) & NNRTIs (non-nucleoside) — stop the reverse transcriptase enzyme
  • Protease inhibitors — block the final assembly enzyme
  • Integrase inhibitors — block insertion of viral DNA into the host genome

All classes work toward the same end point: preventing HIV from multiplying, which lowers viral load and lets the immune system recover and produce healthy CD4 cells.

NRTIs — the one class to know in more depth

Example drugs (not required for the matrix): zidovudine, abacavir, tenofovir. Typically given in pairs. MOA: inhibits reverse transcriptase, blocking the virus's ability to incorporate its RNA into host DNA.

Black box warning

Lactic acidosis syndrome — lactate levels are typically monitored for the first couple of months after starting therapy. Common adverse effects: peripheral neuropathy, pancreatitis, lipoatrophy, hepatic steatosis. Relatively few drug-drug interactions overall.

Treatment principles

  • Start as soon as possible after diagnosis
  • Goal: decrease viral load to an undetectable level
  • Guided by CD4 count, viral load, and patient preference
  • Monitored via CD4 count (higher = healthier immune system) and viral load (lower = better) — an undetectable viral load carries effectively no transmission risk, but the person is still HIV-positive

PrEP vs. PEP

PrEP — pre-exposure prophylaxisPEP — post-exposure prophylaxis
When it's used Before exposure — for someone in an ongoing relationship with an HIV-positive partner After a known/suspected exposure (occupational or non-occupational)
How Daily antiretroviral medication (often a two-in-one pill); requires a detailed sexual/drug-use history and barrier discussion ART regimen for 28 days
Effectiveness / follow-up Reduces transmission risk by >90%; not given broadly, since inappropriate use raises resistance risk HIV testing at baseline, 6 weeks, and 12 weeks after exposure
