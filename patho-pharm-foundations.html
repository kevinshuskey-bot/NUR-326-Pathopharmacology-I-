Pharmacology Foundations

The vocabulary and mechanics behind every drug class you'll study — what a drug goes through in the body, how it's classified, and the safety systems built around prescribing.

Three phases of drug activity

Phase 1

Pharmaceutic

Ingestion → disintegration → dissolution → absorption. All oral drugs must dissolve before they can be absorbed; formulation (ODT, liquid, capsule, enteric-coated) determines how fast that happens.

Phase 2

Pharmacokinetic

What the body does to the drug: Absorption → Distribution → Metabolism → Excretion.

Phase 3

Pharmacodynamic

What the drug does to the body — its mechanism of action and therapeutic effect.

Absorption & routes

  • Enteral — via the GI tract (oral, sublingual, buccal, rectal). Enteric-coated tablets are designed to dissolve in the small intestine, not the stomach.
  • Parenteral — outside the GI tract (SQ, IM, IV, intrathecal, epidural). IV is fastest — no absorption barrier, often irreversible once given.
  • Topical — applied to eyes, skin, ears, nose, lungs.
Bioavailability & first-pass effect

IV = 100% bioavailability. Oral drugs are always <100% because anything absorbed from the GI tract passes through the liver first — a portion is inactivated before it ever reaches systemic circulation.

Distribution

Movement of drug from the bloodstream to its site of action — depends on adequate blood flow. Decreased perfusion (PVD, abscesses, tumors) means decreased distribution.

Protein binding — classic example: warfarin

Warfarin is 97–99% protein-bound; only 1–3% circulates free and active. In a patient with hypoalbuminemia (malnutrition, liver disease), less drug is bound → more free, active warfarin → increased bleeding risk.

Blood-brain barrier: tight capillary junctions block most drugs; only lipid-soluble drugs or those with a transport system cross (alcohol and glucose can cross freely). Not fully developed in infants.

Metabolism & the CYP450 system

The liver converts lipid-soluble drugs into water-soluble metabolites so the kidney can excrete them. Most of this happens via cytochrome P-450 (CYP450) enzymes.

Substrate

Uses CYP450

A pro-drug is a substrate that CYP450 converts into its active form.

Inducer

Speeds up metabolism

Less drug in the body → reduced therapeutic effect.

Inhibitor

Slows metabolism

More drug in the body → increased toxicity risk. Grapefruit juice is a classic CYP450 inhibitor — avoid it 2–4 hours around dosing.

Excretion & half-life

Kidney is the major excretion site (glomerular filtration, tubular secretion/reabsorption). Renal dysfunction → drugs accumulate → toxicity. GFR (from creatinine, age, size, sex) is the best measure of kidney function.

Half-life (T½)

Time for serum concentration to drop by 50%. It takes about 5 half-lives to eliminate ~97% of a drug, and about 4–5 half-lives to reach steady state (intake = elimination). T½ determines dosing interval.

Pharmacodynamics: receptors

  • Agonist — binds a receptor and activates it (e.g., isoproterenol, a beta-1 agonist → vasodilation).
  • Antagonist — binds and blocks a receptor (e.g., diphenhydramine blocks H1; propranolol blocks beta-1).
  • Receptor-less activation — some drugs work by direct chemical action, not receptor binding (antacids neutralize acid directly; magnesium sulfate works osmotically as a laxative).

Drug safety systems

Narrow therapeutic index (NTI) drugs

Small margin between therapeutic and toxic levels — require monitoring. Classic examples: theophylline, digoxin, phenobarbital, lithium, warfarin.

High-alert medications (ISMP)

Most likely to cause serious harm if used in error: insulin, heparin, opioids, IV potassium chloride, neuromuscular blockers, chemotherapy.

Error-prevention practices: tall-man lettering (HePARin), computerized administration systems, leading zeros (0.25 mg, never .25 mg), no trailing zeros (1 mg, never 1.0 mg), patient involvement in their own therapy.

Drug interactions

  • Additive — two drugs, similar MOA (2 antibiotics for a complicated infection).
  • Synergism/potentiation — two drugs, different MOA, combined effect > either alone (warfarin + aspirin).
  • Displacement — one drug knocks another off plasma protein-binding sites, increasing the displaced drug's effect.

Drug approval, classification & controlled substances

FDA approval path: preclinical (animals) → Phase I (healthy volunteers) → Phase II (patients with the disease) → Phase III (large market release) → Phase IV (continued post-market evaluation).

Teratogens & pregnancy categories

Alcohol, marijuana, and nicotine are all known teratogens. Old FDA risk categories ran A (safe) through X (known risk that outweighs any benefit) — since replaced by the Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling Rule (PLLR), which requires a narrative risk summary instead of a letter grade.
