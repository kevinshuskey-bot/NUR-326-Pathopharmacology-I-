Fluid & Electrolytes

Where fluid sits, what pulls it where, and the six electrolyte imbalances you'll be tested on again and again.

Fluid compartments & movement

~50–60% of body weight is water. Intracellular = 70% of body water; extracellular = 30% (interstitial + intravascular/plasma).

Osmosis

Water moves

Low solute → high solute concentration, across a semipermeable membrane.

Diffusion

Molecules move

High → low concentration until equal.

Hydrostatic pressure

Pushes fluid OUT

Generated by blood pressure — pushes water out of the capillary into interstitial space.

Oncotic (colloid osmotic) pressure

Pulls fluid IN

From plasma proteins (mainly albumin) — pulls fluid from tissue back into the vascular space.

Where electrolytes live

IntracellularExtracellular
Potassium, Magnesium, PhosphorusSodium, Chloride, Bicarbonate, Calcium

Normal values: Na 136–145 · K 3.5–5.0 · Mg 1.3–2.1 · Ca 9.0–10.5 · Phosphate 3–4.5

Sodium — "water follows sodium"

Hyponatremia (<136)Hypernatremia (>145)
CausesGI losses, diuretics, adrenal insufficiency, burns, water intoxicationExcess sodium intake, inadequate water intake, profound diuresis
S/SConfusion, anorexia, muscle weakness → seizures/comaConfusion, seizures, extreme thirst, dry sticky mucous membranes, muscle cramps

Dilutional hyponatremia = hypervolemic; depletional = hypovolemic. Sodium bicarbonate: raises pH and sodium — PO preferred, IV is a vesicant at high concentration.

Potassium

Hypokalemia (<3.5) & hyperkalemia (>5.0) both threaten the heart

Both cause cardiac rhythm disturbances that can be lethal. Hypokalemia also causes muscle weakness, cramps, decreased bowel motility. Hyperkalemia causes muscle weakness and GI cramping/diarrhea.

IV potassium chloride is never a push

Undiluted IV KCl can cause fatal ventricular fibrillation. Must be diluted, given slowly (no more than 10–20 mEq/hr, no more than 40 mEq/L concentration), and only with documented urine output. Contraindicated in renal failure/dialysis — always question that order.

Hyperkalemia treatment: Kayexalate (binds K in the gut, exchanges for Na — watch for bowel necrosis in patients with abnormal bowel function), or D50 + insulin (shifts K into cells temporarily).

Magnesium, calcium & phosphate

LowHigh
Magnesium (1.3–2.1)Hyperactive reflexes, confusion, cramps, tremors, seizures. Treat with oral/IV magnesium.Lethargy, floppiness, weakness, decreased reflexes, flushed skin, ↓pulse/BP
Calcium (9.0–10.5)Tetany, laryngeal spasm, prolonged QT; Chvostek's (facial twitch) and Trousseau's (carpal spasm with BP cuff) signsActs as a sedative — fatigue, confusion, weakness → seizures/coma, kidney stones
Phosphate (3–4.5)Tremor, confusion, seizure, muscle weakness, bone painUsually asymptomatic; presents as symptoms of the resulting hypocalcemia

Calcium and phosphate move in opposite directions — low calcium tends to mean high phosphate. IV calcium chloride/gluconate is given centrally. Hypercalcemia treatment: hydration, diuretics + NaCl, dialysis if renal failure.
