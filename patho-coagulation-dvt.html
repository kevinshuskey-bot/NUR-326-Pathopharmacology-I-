Patho Review / Topic 5

Coagulation & DVT

How a clot actually forms, how each anticoagulant/antiplatelet interrupts that process at a different step, and what makes a clot in a deep vein dangerous.

The clotting cascade

Platelets (normal 150,000–400,000/mm³; thrombocytopenia <100,000) circulate freely until vessel injury triggers adhesion → degranulation → aggregation → activation of the clotting cascade. The cascade is a group of proteins (clotting factors) that, once activated, form a fibrin meshwork trapping platelets = a clot (hemostasis).

How the body stops clotting on its own

Antithrombin III inhibits thrombin. Tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI) inhibits factor Xa. The fibrinolytic system then breaks down (lyses) the clot once it's no longer needed.

The universal risk

Every anticoagulant's biggest adverse effect is bleeding — internal or external. Know why the patient is on it and where they're at risk. Monitor H&H, vital signs, bruising, petechiae, black tarry stools.

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) / VTE

Virchow's triad

Three factors that promote venous thrombosis: venous stasis, venous endothelial damage, and hypercoagulable states. Highest risk: ortho surgery, spinal cord injury, OB/GYN conditions.

A thrombus usually forms near a venous valve; inflammation promotes inappropriate platelet aggregation and growth — causing the pain/redness, though a DVT can also be completely asymptomatic. Significant obstruction → edema of the extremity.

Complications

Most serious: pulmonary embolism. Also: chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension, post-thrombotic syndrome, phlegmasia cerulea dolens (painful blue swelling).

Treatment/prevention: anticoagulation, IVC filters, embolectomy, SCDs, TED hose, early mobilization.
