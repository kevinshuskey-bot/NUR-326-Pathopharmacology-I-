Week 1 / Topic 1

Antimicrobial Principles

How we describe, choose, dose, and monitor antimicrobials before getting into individual drug classes — the vocabulary and decision logic everything else in this course builds on.

Key terms

These get used constantly for the rest of the course — worth having cold before class.

  • BactericidalKills the bacteria outright.
  • BacteriostaticOnly inhibits bacterial growth; the immune system finishes the job.
  • Broad spectrumEffective against many organism types (gram +/–) — the "shotgun" approach, used when the organism isn't identified yet.
  • Narrow spectrumEffective against a few specific species — the "BB gun" approach, used once the organism and sensitivities are known.
  • Selective toxicityToxic to the target cell while sparing nearby normal cells.
  • ResistanceAbility of an organism to survive an antimicrobial or render it ineffective. Can be innate (always been that way) or acquired (the pathogen mutated).
  • SuperinfectionA new infection that develops during treatment for a primary infection — e.g., C. difficile or candidiasis after a course of antibiotics.
  • Prophylactic useAntibiotics given to prevent infection in high-risk situations — high-risk surgery (ortho, cardiac, abdominal), dental work in patients at risk for endocarditis, or immunocompromised/neutropenic patients.

Mechanisms of action — the big five

Every antimicrobial class this semester falls into one of these buckets. This is the organizing spine of the whole course.

1

Inhibit cell wall synthesis

Weakens the wall; fluid rushes in, the cell swells and bursts.

2

Increase cell wall permeability

Makes the wall easier for the drug (or the immune system) to get through.

3

Inhibit protein synthesis — kills

Stops protein production in a way that's lethal to the cell.

4

Inhibit protein synthesis — static

Stops the bacteria from functioning/reproducing without killing it outright.

5

Inhibit/alter DNA synthesis

Or disrupts a specific metabolic/biochemical reaction the organism needs.

How an antibiotic gets chosen

  • Community- vs. hospital-acquired (nosocomial). Community-acquired infections are typically easier to treat and more often viral; hospital-acquired infections are more often bacterial and more resistant.
  • Susceptibility patterns — what the suspected organism is known to respond to.
  • Site of infection — can the drug actually penetrate there? Some antimicrobials concentrate in the lungs, some in the kidneys/urine, some cross into bone or CSF more easily than others.
  • Suspected organism. Matching the drug to the organism is the single biggest lever for limiting resistance.
The goal is always cultures first, antibiotics second — that's the ideal, even though it isn't always what happens in practice.Module 1 voiceover

Cultures

Always try to obtain cultures before starting antimicrobials — once the drug is on board, growth may be suppressed and the organism becomes harder to identify.

Exceptions — start empiric therapy first
  • Critically ill patient where cultures would delay treatment too long
  • Meningitis — start something that crosses the blood-brain barrier
  • Severe sepsis

A culture order is a stat priority, not something that waits for a convenient moment. For blood cultures, draw two sets — one aerobic, one anaerobic bottle — and always prefer a peripheral stick over a central line (central lines/ports carry heavy surface bacterial load and are a common source of contaminated cultures). Clean the skin and bottle tops thoroughly.

Reading sensitivity results

Disk-diffusion test

Antibiotic-soaked disks are placed on a plate smeared with the organism. A clear zone of no growth around a disk means that antibiotic is effective; growth right up to the disk means it isn't.

Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) & Minimum Bactericidal Concentration (MBC)

Organisms are grown in tubes with increasing drug concentrations. MIC is the lowest concentration that stops growth (doesn't necessarily kill). MBC is the lowest concentration that kills ≥99.9% of the colony. In practice, results are usually just reported as susceptible or resistant — but the principle matters: give the lowest effective amount, since more drug isn't better once the organism is already susceptible at a lower dose (more side effects, faster resistance).

Patient characteristics that shape response

Allergies

The first question is always what the "allergy" actually was. Nausea/vomiting is an adverse effect, not an allergy. Rash, hives, and angioedema/anaphylaxis are true allergic reactions.

Cross-reactivity

A patient truly allergic to one penicillin is considered allergic to all penicillins. Penicillin allergy also carries some cross-reactivity risk with cephalosporins (roughly 1–4%) — avoid cephalosporins altogether if the penicillin reaction was anaphylactic. Sulfa allergies (e.g., to Bactrim) are also common and worth flagging separately.

Age, renal & liver function

Age extremes (very young, very old) generally need lower concentrations — the body is less able to metabolize/clear the drug. Poor renal or hepatic function limits how well a patient tolerates antimicrobials in general, and many of the "big gun" antibiotics used for serious infections are also the hardest on the kidneys.

Dose & length of treatment

  • Host defense — a robust immune system can do some of the work itself.
  • Site of infection — an isolated, walled-off abscess takes longer to clear than a UTI, where the drug reaches the site easily.
  • Organism — how resistant or virulent it is.
  • Severity — local vs. systemic infection (e.g., sepsis).
  • Time vs. concentration — some drugs depend on hitting a specific level (peak/trough monitoring); others depend on sustained time above a threshold.

Infections in the healthcare setting

Nosocomial infections are acquired within a healthcare facility, and healthcare-setting organisms tend to be more virulent and more drug-resistant.

Single-drug resistance

MRSA

Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus.

Class resistance

CRE

Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae.

Multi-drug resistance

MDRO

Resistant to multiple drugs/classes at once.

ESKAPE organisms — the classic multidrug-resistant set

Enterococcus faecium · Staphylococcus aureus · Klebsiella pneumoniae · Acinetobacter baumannii · Pseudomonas aeruginosa · Enterobacter spp.

Risk goes up with medical illness (immune response suppressed), surgery/invasive procedures, and anything that breaches a normal barrier — IV catheters (CLABSI), urinary catheters (CAUTI), NG tubes. Post-op patients are also watched for atelectasis/pneumonia, surgical wound infection, wound dehiscence, and UTI.

Resistance & the environment

Resistance develops through innate traits, sharing of genetic material between organisms, or mutation under drug exposure — classic survival of the fittest, especially when a course isn't taken long enough to kill off the full population.

Environmental persistence
  • C. diff spores can survive 5+ months on surfaces (some reports: years)
  • VRE can contaminate cushions/surfaces and spread to other patients
  • Reported contamination rates: 37–65% of HCW gowns after caring for a VRE/MRSA patient; 69% of white coats after examining a colonized patient, with 27% hand contamination after touching the coat

Why treatment fails

  • Wrong drug chosen, started too late, dosed too low, or not continued long enough
  • Infection appears to improve, then returns — sometimes more virulent
  • Drug simply can't reach the infection site (isolated abscess, brain if it doesn't cross the blood-brain barrier)
Antibiotic prescribing pressure

Most upper respiratory infections/bronchitis are viral. The odds an antibiotic actually helps an acute URI are roughly 1 in 4,000 — versus roughly 1 in 1,000 odds it sends the patient to the ED from an adverse reaction (rash, allergic reaction, diarrhea).
