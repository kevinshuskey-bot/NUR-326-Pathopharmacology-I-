Antiviral Pharmacology

A much smaller toolbox than antibacterials, and for a structural reason: viruses live inside your own cells, so anything that kills the virus risks killing the host cell too.

Why so few antivirals?

  1. By the time symptoms show up, the virus has often already finished replicating.
  2. Antivirals only work during active replication.
  3. Viruses live inside your own cells — a drug potent enough to kill the virus can also kill the healthy host cell.

Antivirals are a relatively new class (1990s) and remain effective against only a handful of viruses: HSV, HZV, influenza A/B, RSV, hepatitis, HIV, and CMV.

How they work — three mechanisms

1

Interfere with nucleic acid synthesis

Disrupts DNA and/or RNA synthesis or its regulation.

2

Block viral entry

Prevents the virus from binding to/entering the cell, which stops replication before it starts.

3

Stimulate the immune system

Boosts the body's own ability to clear the virus.

Routes vary widely: oral (tablet/liquid), topical cream/ointment, and IV.

Herpes family: acyclovir, valacyclovir, famciclovir

acyclovir (Zovirax) · valacyclovir (Valtrex) · famciclovir (Famvir)

Suppresses replication of HSV-1 (oral), HSV-2 (genital), and VZV (herpes zoster/chickenpox) — reduces symptom severity, outbreak frequency, and viral shedding for both initial and recurrent infection. Available PO, IV, and topical.

Not a cure

Disease can recur, and multiple treatment courses may be needed over time. Adverse effects: GI distress, renal impairment, seizures, ITP. IV form causes tissue necrosis if it infiltrates — confirm line patency.

Herpes epidemiology worth knowing
  • ~1% of the US population has genital herpes; twice as common in women
  • HSV-1 generally occurs above the waist; HSV-2 below
  • Herpes zoster affects roughly 1 in 3 Americans
  • First zoster vaccine (Zostavax/ZVL, live) approved 2006; newer recombinant vaccine (Shingrix/RZV) approved 2017 for ages 50+ — reduces risk and severity of outbreaks

Influenza: oseltamivir (Tamiflu)

Mostly active against influenza A, with some activity against B. MOA: inhibits neuraminidase, an enzyme influenza viruses need to spread between cells. Used both prophylactically and to treat active disease — most effective within 48 hours of symptom onset. Most often given to elderly/immunocompromised patients after a known exposure. FDA-approved for H1N1 treatment in April 2009. Oral only.

Adverse effects

Nausea/vomiting; seizures; renal impairment.

CMV: ganciclovir (Zirgan)

Treats cytomegalovirus in immunocompromised patients (AIDS, transplant, immunosuppressed) — controls the virus but does not cure it. MOA: inhibits viral DNA polymerase, causing chain termination. Available IV and PO.

Black box warnings

Hematologic toxicity, fertility impairment, fetal toxicity, carcinogenesis. Do not combine with imipenem-cilastatin (seizure risk). Watch renal function when combined with other nephrotoxic drugs.
